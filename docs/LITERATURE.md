# Literature map

Five bodies of work feed the model. The table shows what each one gives the model and which equations it supports (see [EQUATIONS.md](EQUATIONS.md)).

**The gap.** The studies price solar either from the owner's side or from the customer's side. None of the work reviewed here solves for a lease price that must satisfy a school's saving, the company's equity return and the bank's coverage test at the same time, under Delhi's annual net-metering rule, with every input treated as uncertain. That two-sided, probabilistic price band is what this project adds.

## References

- Leasing Rooftop Solar to a School: What the Literature Shows, and What It Leaves Out
- 1. Introduction
- My research question is whether zero-upfront solar leasing is financially feasible for a private day school in Delhi, and for the company that leases it. Under this model a Renewable Energy Service Company (RESCO) pays for the system, installs it on the school’s roof, owns it, and maintains it. The school pays nothing upfront and buys the electricity the panels make at an agreed price per unit, under a long contract called a power purchase agreement (PPA). The question has two sides. The school will only sign if it saves money, and the company will only build if it earns enough to repay its bank and reward its owners. So the real question is whether a price exists that works for both.
- No single body of research answers this. The answer sits between five areas of literature, and this review takes them in order. It will first look at research on solar as a service, which explains what the model is and which policies make or break it. It will then look at studies of schools that already have solar, what they show and what they leave out. The third section covers the rule for surplus power, which decides what a unit of solar electricity is worth. The fourth looks at the two parties and their two ledgers, meaning lease versus buy for the school, and the return and the bank’s test for the company. The fifth looks at how researchers handle uncertainty, moving from single estimates to probabilities. The review ends with the gap between these five areas, which is where this capstone sits, and with a table that shows which part of my model each area supports.
- 2. Solar as a service: once the hardware works, the contract decides who gets it
- 2.1 What the service model is
- In most of the world, a building owner who wants solar has to buy it. Third-party ownership changes that. Lazo et al. (2025) review 70 studies and policy reports on the model and define it clearly. A company finances, owns, operates, and maintains the system, and the host pays either a fixed lease fee or a price per unit of electricity under a PPA, usually for 15 to 25 years (pp. 2, 6). The host needs no capital and carries little technical risk. In India this arrangement is called the RESCO or OPEX model. Sarangi and Taghizadeh-Hesary (2021) describe its pricing logic in one line, the developer sells power to the roof owner “at a tariff less than the grid tariff, but which would still allow the RESCO to maintain a margin.” That one line contains my whole research question. The price has to sit below what the school pays the grid, and above what the company needs.
- Hardware still matters. The cost of the panels, their efficiency, and their upkeep decide whether solar can pay at all, and all of them enter my model as inputs. The argument in this literature is narrower. Once the hardware is proven and affordable, what decides whether a particular customer gets solar is the contract, meaning who owns the system, who pays for it, and who carries the risk. Överholm (2015) makes this case from 50 interviews with five pioneering third-party firms in the United States and the partners around them. In his account the work of these firms was not making better panels. It was arranging investors, insurance, and contracts, and the investors who owned the systems often chose the hardware brands themselves, so close partnerships with manufacturers were not needed.
- One detail in his study speaks directly to this capstone. Two of the five firms took a private school as their very first customer (Överholm, 2015, Table 2, p. 73), and all five preferred a customer that both owns and uses its building, because that kept the contract simple (p. 72). A private day school that owns its campus is this kind of host.
- 2.2 The policies that make or break it
- Överholm (2015) sorts policies into those the model cannot live without and those that only help. Two are critical. The first is that the law must allow a company that is not a utility to sell or lease power to a customer. Lazo et al. (2025) agree, and call the legal status of the model the main challenge to its spread (p. 13). The second is that the customer must be able to use the solar power on site and settle the rest through net metering. Överholm’s reasoning is practical. Net metering means that systems “do not have to be exactly dimensioned for the load of the site” (p. 74). He also finds that feed-in tariffs, which pay only for power sold to the grid, work against the service model, because the customer cannot buy the solar power and claim the tariff at the same time (pp. 73–74, 76). Tax credits and other financial support come third in his list, helpful but not critical. He is careful to present all of this as hypotheses from interviews, which need testing in other markets (p. 77).
- Delhi meets both critical conditions on paper. Its regulations allow a third party to own the generator, and they settle rooftop solar through net metering (Delhi Electricity Regulatory Commission [DERC], 2014, Regs. 2(17), 9). Whether the model then pays at Delhi’s prices is the open question.
- 2.3 Who it reaches, and what holds it back
- The service model is often presented as a way to widen access, and the evidence partly supports this. O’Shaughnessy and Barbose (2026) study residential systems installed across the United States from 2017 to 2024 and find that customers with third-party-owned systems earned about 16 to 18 percent less, at the median, than customers who paid for their systems themselves. They also find that third-party ownership regained market share after 2022, when rising interest rates made solar loans more expensive. Both findings matter for a school. A school with no spare capital is the customer this model is meant to reach, and borrowing in India is expensive.
- The model does not travel easily. Lazo et al. (2025) note that it remains underused in developing countries, held back by costly finance, uncertain regulation, high transaction costs for small systems, and the credit risk of customers (p. 2). Yamashiro and Mori (2023) study a zero-upfront lease for solar panels and batteries on Miyakojima Island in Japan, and they show a second problem. The company priced the lease so that customers got only a “marginal bill saving” (p. 4), and sold the service on backup power during typhoons instead. A service model can exist, and still leave very little on the table for the customer.
- In India the model has grown. Climate Investment Funds and PwC (2018) report that the third-party share of the rooftop market rose from 3 percent in 2012 to 29 percent in 2017 (p. 28), and they name the limited availability of finance as one of the biggest reasons rooftop solar has been slow (p. 33). The closest study to my own is Arora et al. (2025), who interviewed 22 government officials, school staff, developers, and distribution company staff about RESCO systems on four government schools in Delhi. School officials reported that their electricity bills fell by 30 to 40 percent. The process took much longer than the policy allowed, and developers said that finance for RESCO projects was hard to get. This is valuable ground reality, but it has limits for my question. The schools were government schools that joined through a state tender, the savings were reported and not measured, and the study gives no price, no cost, and no return for the developer.
- This is the pattern across the whole area. The literature on solar as a service describes the model, the contract terms, and the policies, but it does not calculate whether a given deal works. Lazo et al. (2025) close their review by naming the modelling of third-party portfolios and the optimizing of contract terms as work still to be done. That is the first part of the gap.
- 3. Schools with solar: what the studies show, and what they leave out
- A second body of work studies rooftop solar on schools and other education buildings directly. These studies come from New Zealand, Palestine, Kuwait, Bangladesh, and several parts of India. I read them for three lessons.
- 3.1 The school calendar creates a surplus
- A school uses power on a fixed schedule. It is open for part of the day, closed on weekends, and closed for long vacations, while the panels keep working whenever the sun shines. Emmanuel et al. (2017), who studied a metered 10 kWp system at a primary school in Wellington, state it directly: “the excess power generated at low load condition during holidays and weekends is exported to the grid” (p. 573). Al-Otaibi et al. (2015) measured two school rooftops in Kuwait for a year and plot the hourly load of a school on an open day and on a closed day, and the closed-day load is only a small fraction of the open-day load. Baruah et al. (2025) model a government secondary school in Sikkim and count 118 days in the year on which the school needs no power at all. Satsangi et al. (2019) logged a 40 kWp system on an education building in Agra, about 200 km from Delhi, for a year, and their Sunday record shows the battery filling up during the day because the institute is closed.
- The same studies show how much depends on the size of the system compared with what the school uses. In Podder et al. (2021), the best of four sizes for an academic building in Bangladesh covers 97 percent of the building’s yearly use and still feeds a further 26 percent of what it generates to the grid. In Baruah et al. (2025), the array is sized to fill the roof and not to match the load, and most of what it makes is sold back to the grid. So for a school the amount of surplus is not a detail. It is one of the main results, and it depends on the calendar and on the system size chosen.
- 3.2 Measured output falls below simulated output
- Most feasibility studies estimate output with simulation software. Three of the school and institutional studies also measured it, and in all three the meter came in below the simulation. Sharma et al. (2023) measured 17,799 kWh in a year from a 13 kWp system on an institutional building in Bhubaneswar, against 21,522 kWh simulated, about 17 percent lower. Across the three Palestinian schools in Ibrik and Hashaika (2019), measured output was roughly 17 to 29 percent below the expected output, by my calculation from their yearly figures. At the Wellington school the measured yield was 2.99 hours a day, about 1,090 kWh per kWp a year, while the financial analysis in the same paper used 1,616 kWh per kWp (Emmanuel et al., 2017), a gap of about 32 percent.
- This matters because the economics in several of these papers were calculated on the simulated figure, so the reported paybacks are likely too optimistic. For my model it means the yield cannot be one number taken from software. It has to be a range, and that range should sit below the simulated value.
- 3.3 One owner, one number
- All of these studies look at the school or the institution as the owner. The systems were bought by the institution, paid for by a grant, or donated, and none of the studies models a company that owns the system and sells power to the school. They also report single estimates. Podder et al. (2021) report a payback of 8.3 years, Sharma et al. (2023) report 9.8 years, and Ibrik and Hashaika (2019) report under 5 years. The nearest case to Delhi, a private school campus in Greater Noida studied by Boruah and Chandel (2024), reports 6.15 years for a 200 kWp system with a battery. These figures are useful benchmarks. But each one is a single number, for a single owner, under a single set of assumptions. None says how likely the result is, and none says what a leasing company would need. That is the second part of the gap.
- 4. The rule for surplus power decides what the system is worth
- 4.1 Net metering and net billing
- What happens to the surplus is not a technical matter. It is set by regulation, and it differs from place to place. Chandel et al. (2022) compare rooftop solar policies across Indian states and show that they differ in the type of metering, in the size limits, and in the period over which surplus is settled. Under net metering, a unit sent to the grid cancels a unit taken from the grid later, so it is worth the full retail price. Under net billing, exported units are paid at a lower price. Ordóñez et al. (2022) write out the billing equations for both rules, using Ecuador, which has net metering with two years of rolling credit, and Spain, which has net billing. They show that the two rules push owners in different directions. Net metering does not reward producing more than the owner consumes, while net billing allows a larger system because the surplus always earns something, only at a lower price.
- Two studies show how large the effect can be. Laksono et al. (2026) take one simulated 70.2 kWp system on an Indonesian campus and value it twice. When exports are credited at the full tariff, the system pays back in 8.5 years. Under Indonesia’s current rule, which gives exports no value, only the 14.9 percent of output used on site counts, and the system does not recover its cost. Boruah and Chandel (2024) show the same thing next door to Delhi. Uttar Pradesh does not allow commercial consumers to use net metering, and pays for their exports at the distribution company’s average power purchase cost, ₹4.89 per unit, against an average tariff of ₹8.86 (p. 2). For their school, energy costs ₹4.21 per unit with a payback of 6.15 years under net metering, and ₹5.33 with a payback of 7.21 years without it. The same panels on the same roof are worth different amounts on the two sides of a state border.
- 4.2 Why most studies miss this
- The rule only matters to a model that separates the power used on site from the power exported, and many models do not. Sommerfeldt and Madani (2017a) find that 66 percent of the 51 studies they reviewed did not consider the building’s load at all, and 57 percent valued all solar electricity at a single price. Satsangi et al. (2019) make a similar point about India, that most studies of self-consumption are about homes and are simulated, not measured, which is why they measured it at an institutional building. Under a net billing rule, ignoring the load is a serious error, since a unit used on site and a unit exported are worth different amounts. Under net metering the error is smaller, because an exported unit comes back later at full value. It does not disappear where the tariff changes with the time of day.
- 4.3 What this means for Delhi
- Delhi’s rule sits between the two cases. Exported units are banked and set against later imports within the financial year, and whatever credit is left on 31 March is paid at a lower rate (DERC, 2014, Reg. 9). So in Delhi the quantity that matters most is the yearly surplus, the units the panels make beyond what the school uses in a year, and that is exactly the quantity that the school calendar and the system size drive. The only published financial analysis of third-party ownership under Delhi’s net metering rules is Narula and Reddy (2015), and it studies households at 2014 prices. I have found no study that applies Delhi’s yearly banking rule to a school’s calendar. Regulations also change, which is one more reason to treat the export rule as a scenario in the model and not as fixed background.
- 5. Two parties, two ledgers
- 5.1 The school’s side: lease or buy
- A school that wants solar has two choices, buy the system or sign a lease or PPA. Finance has an old and clear way to compare them. Franks and Hodges (1978) show that the value of a lease to the user equals the purchase price it avoids, minus the loan that the same stream of payments could support (p. 662). They also work through the case of a user that cannot use tax benefits (p. 664). A not-for-profit school may be in that position, although the legal form of the school I study still has to be confirmed.
- Bolinger (2009) applies this thinking to solar in the United States. For a host that pays no tax, he finds that owning the system outright needs revenue of about $0.57 per kWh, while a PPA with a tax-paying owner needs about $0.35, because the owner can use tax benefits that the host cannot. For a creditworthy non-profit, he concludes, a PPA “seems to be an obvious choice.” Feldman and Margolis (2014) add the other half of the picture. For a commercial host with a 10 percent discount rate, owning is cheaper than a PPA, about $0.10 against $0.16 per kWh, because third-party capital costs more. The two only become equal when the host’s discount rate reaches 23 percent. So which option is cheaper depends on the host’s own cost of money, and on whether it can use the tax benefits. These are American results under American tax law, and the sizes will not transfer to India. The logic does, and it means my model has to value the buy option next to the lease, using the school’s own discount rate.
- There is also evidence that hosts do not decide on present value alone. Sharp (1983) interviewed municipal administrators about their lease and purchase decisions, and the factor they named most often was the annual budget constraint. A school’s management may think the same way, which helps explain why zero upfront cost is attractive even where buying looks cheaper on paper.
- 5.2 The company’s side: the owners and the bank
- The company has its own ledger. It pays for the system, usually with about 70 percent borrowed money and 30 percent from its owners (Climate Investment Funds & PwC, 2018, p. 54). It then collects the PPA price on every unit, and pays for upkeep, tax, and the loan. Two tests decide whether it will build. The first is the owners’ return. Jena et al. (2016), in a design study for a financing facility for Indian RESCO developers, report that developers borrow at 10.5 to 14 percent and look for a return on their own money of about 15 percent. Their base case project earns 14.1 percent, close to that mark, and the return falls to as little as 5.6 percent in their stress cases, where customers pay less than agreed or default. The second is the bank’s test. A lender wants the cash left after costs to cover each year’s loan payment with a cushion. Rarasati et al. (2026) show how that cash shrinks over a project’s life as the panels age and upkeep costs rise, by 36.7 percent over 25 years in their example.
- Narula and Reddy (2015) is the only peer-reviewed model I have found of a third-party owner’s finances in Delhi. For households under the 2014 rules, they find that the third party’s return rises with system size, about 7 percent for 2.5 kWp, 12 percent for 5 kWp, and 18 percent for 10 kWp. When the household is given a 10 percent discount on the grid tariff, the return on the 10 kWp system falls to 14.7 percent. This is the two-sided problem in one table. Every rupee of saving given to the customer comes out of the company’s return. Their prices are from 2014, their customer is a household, and some of the rules they model have changed, so the numbers cannot be used as they are. The structure can.
- Bolinger (2009) explains why both tests are needed. A price can give the owners their return over the life of a project and still leave a year in which the cash does not cover the loan. Halimatussadiah et al. (2023) offer a clean way to turn all of this into a price. For planned solar and wind projects in Indonesia, they solve for the electricity price at which a project’s net present value is exactly zero, and then measure how much each incentive moves that price. The strongest one they test lowers the price a solar project needs by about 14 percent. Asking for the price that just works, instead of asking for the profit at a given price, is the approach I take for both parties.
- 5.3 A price that works for both
- Very few studies put the two ledgers side by side. Metawe and Brent (2026) do, for apartments in Cairo. They model a company that needs a 19.5 percent return, and lower the PPA price step by step to find where that return fails. For households with high electricity use a deal exists, with customer savings of up to 19 percent against the grid price. For a medium-use household, the company would need to charge about 16 percent more than the grid tariff, so no customer would sign. Junlakarn and Kokchang (2020) reach a similar negative result for a household in Bangkok. For the developer to earn 10 percent it has to charge 18.78 US cents per unit, and at that price the household “did not gain any benefits,” with a net present value of minus $2,531 under the PPA.
- These two papers show that the answer to my question can be no, and that it depends on who the customer is. That is important for a capstone that must not assume its own conclusion. They also show the limits of the existing work. Both study households, and both use a single value for every input. Metawe and Brent also leave out the loan, the tax, and the depreciation that an Indian RESCO’s finances turn on. No study I have found builds both ledgers for a school. That is the third part of the gap.
- 6. From many uncertain numbers to probabilities
- 6.1 Why a single estimate is not enough
- Almost every study in sections 3 to 5 feeds its model one value for each input and reports one value for each result. A 25-year contract does not work like that. Nobody knows what the grid tariff, the yield, or the cost of upkeep will be in year 20. Sommerfeldt and Madani (2017a) reviewed 51 techno-economic studies of rooftop solar published between 2010 and 2015, and documented how common the shortcuts are. Over half (59 percent) used a single yearly production number, 66 percent ignored the building’s load, 57 percent used a single electricity price, and only 14 percent used a Monte Carlo simulation. They conclude that Monte Carlo analysis “shows the most promise” for making these analyses both complete and understandable.
- A Monte Carlo simulation replaces each uncertain input with a range, draws one value from each range, calculates the result, and repeats this thousands of times. The output is not one number but a distribution, which can be read as a probability. Pereira et al. (2014) show why this matters with a small rooftop system on a university building in Brazil. Its average net present value is positive, which a single estimate would report as a success. The simulation shows a 40.81 percent chance that the net present value is negative. A school’s board or a lender would want to know the second number.
- 6.2 How the studies do it
- Heck et al. (2016) give the clearest recipe. They choose a distribution for each input from published estimates, run 10,000 draws, and report the spread of results instead of a single value. Sommerfeldt and Madani (2017b) apply the method to Swedish households in three steps, a deterministic model first, then a sensitivity analysis to find the critical inputs, then a Monte Carlo simulation on those inputs. Their result is stated as a probability of profitability, 8.6 percent without subsidies, and 71.4 or 97.1 percent with them. The same system moves from very unlikely to almost certain depending on policy, and that kind of sentence is one a decision maker can use.
- 6.3 Which inputs matter most
- Khan et al. (2026) bring the method to rooftop solar in South Asia, with a 3.57 MWp system on a factory roof in Bangladesh. They give distributions to project life, panel degradation, electricity price growth, fuel price growth, and upkeep cost, run 2,000 simulations, and then use a global sensitivity analysis to rank the inputs. The growth rate of the electricity price has the strongest effect on net present value and on payback. Syal and MacDonald (2020) use a related method, Sobol indices, to divide the spread in a solar project’s cost of energy among its uncertain inputs. Ranking matters for a practical reason. It tells both parties which numbers to pin down first and which to negotiate hardest.
- All of these studies take the view of one investor. Each asks whether one party’s net present value is positive. None asks how often two parties come out ahead at the same price. That is the fourth part of the gap. It is also where the shortfall in measured output from section 3 belongs, as a range inside the simulation and not as a footnote.
- 7. The gap, and what this capstone adds
- Taken together, the five areas give me almost every piece I need, and none of them puts the pieces together. The literature on solar as a service describes the contract and its policies, but does not calculate a deal. The school studies measure the calendar effect and the yield, but model only an owner, with single estimates. The studies of the surplus rule show that regulation can decide the result, but none applies Delhi’s yearly banking to a school. The financing studies give the school’s lease or buy logic and the company’s two tests, but the only two-sided studies use fixed inputs and study households. The uncertainty studies give the method, but for one party at a time.
- No study I have found asks whether a price exists that works for both a school and a leasing company under Delhi’s rules, and how often it exists once the uncertain inputs are allowed to vary. This capstone asks that question for one private day school. I build two ledgers from the same units of electricity, one for the school and one for the company. From the school’s ledger I find the ceiling, the highest first-year price at which the school still saves. From the company’s ledger I find the floor, the lowest price at which the owners earn their return and the bank is covered. If the floor is below the ceiling, a deal exists. A Monte Carlo simulation then turns that yes or no into a probability, and a sensitivity analysis shows which inputs decide it.
- Either answer is useful. If a price exists in most plausible futures, the result gives schools without spare capital a tested path to solar, and gives a company the terms it would need. If it does not, the result helps explain why few companies offer this service to schools, and points to what would have to change, whether that is cheaper installation, cheaper loans, or a different rule for surplus power. In both cases the frame shifts, from asking whether solar works, to asking for whom it works, at what price, and how often. Table 1 shows how each area of the literature feeds the model.
